# Clear shallows seen from a low eye

From Wikimedia Commons, 26 November 2026, for studying what an eye 0.2 to 2 m above clear shallow water sees
of the floor: how far out it can still be read, how it gives way to the sky's mirror, how sharp it is near and
far, and how the level floor seems to rise towards the horizon. Each is the copy Commons serves for 1600 pixels
across, which is now 1920 pixels (the nearest standard size). None is aerial, from a pier or from a cliff.

Everything in "What it shows" is judged by eye and is an estimate. The camera's height comes from where the
horizon falls, the size of people and wavelets, and what the photographer stood or sat in. "Readable over the
lower 40%" means the floor's markings can be made out from the picture's bottom edge up to 40% of the way to the
horizon line (or to the far shore, where land hides the horizon), measured roughly on the picture.

Twelve are at atolls (Rangiroa, Aitutaki, the Maldives, Majuro, Kiritimati, Kalpeni) and one at a coral
island (Malapascua). The rest are clear tropical shallows elsewhere: Palawan's limestone islands (two), Koh
Adang's sand spit in Thailand (two), Komodo, La Digue's granite boulders, and Bacalar's freshwater lagoon in
Mexico, kept because it is the only one from sitting height under cloud.

## What the set shows as a whole

- From a swimmer's eye (0.1 to 0.5 m) the floor reads over at most the lowest 0 to 25% of the water, even where it is shallow, clear and sunlit; above that the ripple faces mirror the sky. Only a shoal that comes up close under the surface reads further off (La Digue). Waist-high (about 1 m) over very shallow sand it reaches 65 to 80%.
- From standing height over sand 0.1 to 1 m deep in sun, it reads over 40 to 60% as a rule, and all the way to a sandbar when the water is ankle-deep; from a boat looking down and out over a 1 to 3 m reef flat, 45 to 90%, depending most on how far down the camera looks.
- Light ripples under a high sun let the floor read furthest. A breeze's chop breaks up the near floor but hardly shortens the reach. Glassy water with a low or setting sun turns to mirror almost at once (20% or less).
- From a boat, cloud hardly shortened the reach (Komodo 50% against 45 to 55% at El Nido and Bacuit Bay in sun), but it takes the caustics away and turns the far water to a grey mirror. From standing height under cloud ankle-deep sand still reads out to the channel's edge (Aitutaki); from sitting height under cloud the floor vanishes entirely (Bacalar).
- The far water is one of two things. Where the sky can be mirrored it is the sky: pale blue-grey streaks, gold or pink at a low sun. Where the water deepens or is milky it is the water's own colour: turquoise over sand, steel blue over depth, opaque jade in Kiritimati's milky lagoon. In the Aitutaki channel a bright turquoise shines under a grey sky: it needs no sun.
- In sun and ripples the change is gradual over 15 to 25% of the water: a crisp caustic net, then fine compressed lines, then a mottle, then plain tone, then mirror. Every abrupt change in the set follows a change of depth (a channel's edge, a drop-off, a rubble ridge), not distance.
- Near the camera the floor is crisp in sun (bright caustic lines, pebbles); by about a third of the way to the horizon its markings are soft mottles; at the limit only light against dark patches remain.
- With distance the floor's patches and edges are squashed into horizontal streaks, so a level floor looks like a sheet tilted up to meet the horizon (Kalpeni, El Nido, Malapascua). A ridge a few decimetres down reads far beyond the general limit (Kuramathi).
- Refraction at a waterline shows clearly only at the Koh Adang driftwood (roots greener, shorter, displaced); a wader's shins and a granite boulder show little at this size.
